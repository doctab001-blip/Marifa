# Hypokalaemia

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and replacement of hypokalaemia, based on the NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service (SPS) hypokalaemia article and a Scottish emergency medicine guideline "Emergency Management of Hypokalaemia in Adults". No Saudi national hypokalaemia guideline was found; local Saudi practice and formulary must be confirmed. Paediatric care and long-term management are outside this protocol.

## Pathophysiology
Hypokalaemia is a serum potassium below 3.5 mmol/L. [1]
- Potassium is lost through the gut (vomiting, diarrhoea) or kidneys (diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, magnesium depletion).
- Potassium moves into cells with insulin, beta-agonists and alkalosis.
- Low extracellular potassium delays repolarisation, producing U waves, T-wave flattening, ST changes and arrhythmias, most often ventricular tachycardia. [2]
- Magnesium depletion makes hypokalaemia resistant to replacement. [1] [2]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Often none in mild disease. [1]
- Weakness, lethargy, constipation and palpitations in moderate disease. [1]
### Examination signs
- Reduced muscle power and reflexes.
- Irregular pulse.
### Red flags
- Potassium below 2.5 mmol/L (severe), with muscle necrosis and arrhythmias. [1]
- Electrocardiogram (ECG) changes or ventricular tachycardia. [2]
- Digoxin therapy (toxicity risk). [2]
- Cardiac arrest. [2]
### Precipitants
- Vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics, insulin treatment (for example diabetic ketoacidosis), magnesium depletion, poor intake, beta-agonists.
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Hypokalaemic periodic paralysis | Episodic paralysis with normal potassium between attacks |
| Hypomagnesaemia | Low magnesium; hypokalaemia that does not correct |
| Primary hyperaldosteronism | Hypertension with low potassium and metabolic alkalosis |
| Pseudohypokalaemia | Delayed sample with very high white cell count |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Hypokalaemia | Serum potassium below 3.5 mmol/L [1] |
| Mild | 3.0–3.5 mmol/L [1] |
| Moderate | 2.5–2.9 mmol/L [1] |
| Severe | Below 2.5 mmol/L [1] |
| Emergency pathway used by the Scottish guideline | Serum potassium 3.0 mmol/L or below [2] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Get an urgent ECG and check potassium and magnesium. [2]
- Cardiac arrest: start Advanced Life Support, treat hypokalaemia and give magnesium sulphate 10 mmol IV while awaiting the magnesium level. [2]
- Life-threatening hypokalaemia: give IV potassium chloride with monitoring and seek expert help. [2]
- IV potassium must be given in a hospital with appropriate monitoring. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Recheck potassium after every 40 mmol of potassium chloride with normal renal function, or every 20 mmol with severe renal impairment. [2]
- Aim for a potassium of 4.0–4.5 mmol/L. [2]
- Replace magnesium if low; this allows faster correction of potassium. [2]
- Find and treat the cause, and stop or review potassium-losing medicines. [2]
### Escalation
- Arrhythmia or arrest: expert help immediately. [2]
- Rates above 20 mmol/hour need cardiac monitoring. [1]
### Disposition
- Admit for IV replacement, ECG changes or severe or symptomatic hypokalaemia. [1] [2]

### Discharge criteria
- Potassium above 3.5 mmol/L on repeat testing, normal ECG and the cause treated. [2]
### Follow-up
- Repeat potassium and magnesium after discharge or at the next clinic; review diuretics and other causes. [1]
### Patient education
- Why potassium and magnesium matter; take oral potassium with food.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Increase dietary potassium; renal dietitian advice for kidney failure. [2]
### Team referrals
- Renal dietitian and renal team for kidney failure or dialysis patients. [2]
### Learning objectives
- Grade hypokalaemia and recognise ECG changes.
- Choose oral or IV replacement safely, with correct concentration and rate.
- Know when to replace magnesium.
### Key points
- Check magnesium; potassium will not correct without it. [2]
- IV potassium needs monitoring and limited concentration. [1]
- Aim for 4.0–4.5 mmol/L. [2]
### Pitfalls
- Giving concentrated potassium by a peripheral line. [1]
- Giving too much potassium in renal impairment. [2]
- Forgetting to recheck potassium after every 40 mmol (20 mmol in severe renal impairment). [2]
