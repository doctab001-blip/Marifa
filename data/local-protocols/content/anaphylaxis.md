# Anaphylaxis

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and emergency treatment of anaphylaxis, based on the Resuscitation Council UK (RCUK) "Emergency treatment of anaphylactic reactions" guideline (May 2021) as shown in its slide set and the NHS Scotland emergency department summary, and the NHS Lothian anaphylaxis guideline for infusion preparation. Children, anaphylaxis under anaesthesia and long-term allergy management are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction with rapid onset, usually from mast cell and basophil mediator release. [1]
- Airway swelling, bronchospasm, vasodilatation and capillary leak cause hypoxia and shock. [1]
- Common triggers are food, drugs (antibiotics, anaesthetic agents, anti-inflammatory drugs) and insect venom. [1]
- Early intramuscular adrenaline is the first-line treatment. [1] [2]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Rapid onset of skin itch or flushing, throat tightness, wheeze, breathlessness, light-headedness, abdominal pain, vomiting. [1]
### Examination signs
- Urticaria, angioedema, stridor, wheeze, hypotension, tachycardia, pallor. [1]
### Red flags
- Airway swelling or stridor. [1]
- Breathing difficulty, wheeze or oxygen saturation below 92%. [1]
- Hypotension, collapse or shock. [1]
- Persistent problems after two adrenaline doses (refractory anaphylaxis). [2]
### Precipitants
- Food (nuts, shellfish), antibiotics, neuromuscular blockers, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, contrast, latex, insect stings. [1]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Acute severe asthma | No skin changes, no hypotension; history of asthma |
| Vasovagal syncope | Bradycardia, pallor, resolves lying flat; no skin or airway signs |
| Panic attack | Normal oxygen saturation, no swelling or hypotension |
| Hereditary angioedema | Swelling without urticaria; family history; no response to adrenaline |
| Septic shock | Fever and no trigger exposure |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Anaphylaxis | Acute onset of airway, breathing or circulation problems with skin or mucosal change after likely trigger [1] |
| Refractory anaphylaxis | Airway, breathing or circulation problems persisting after two IM adrenaline doses [2] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Call for help; lie the patient flat (or semi-recumbent if breathless); remove trigger if possible. [1]
- Give intramuscular adrenaline at once into the anterolateral thigh; repeat after 5 minutes if no better. [1] [2]
- High-flow oxygen; IV access and fluid bolus for shock. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Monitor oxygen saturation, ECG and blood pressure continuously. [1]
- After two IM doses without response: refractory anaphylaxis; start IV adrenaline infusion with expert help. [2]
- Take tryptase as soon as feasible. [1]
### Escalation
- Refractory anaphylaxis: critical care and anaesthetic help; consider cardiac arrest pathway if pulseless. [2]
### Disposition
- Admit refractory cases and any biphasic risk; observe at least 6 hours after symptom resolution (12 hours after severe or refractory reactions), then discharge with an adrenaline auto-injector and allergy referral. [1]

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | RCUK slide set (2020) [1] | RCUK May 2021 summary [2] | NHS Lothian [3] |
|---|---|---|---|
| IM adrenaline | 500 micrograms (0.5 mL) adult; repeat after 5 minutes | Repeat after 5 minutes | 0.5 mg IM |
| Fluids | 500–1000 mL adults | Early for refractory shock | 20 mL/kg crystalloid |
| Antihistamine | Second-line, no dose | Third-line, non-sedating preferred | Chlorphenamine 10 mg |
| Corticosteroid | Second-line, no dose | Not advised routinely | Hydrocortisone 200 mg |
| Infusion | Experts only | Basis of refractory treatment | 3 mg in 50 mL glucose 5%, 0.1 micrograms/kg/minute |
### Discharge criteria
- Symptom-free after the observation period, adrenaline auto-injector prescribed and trained, allergy referral made and written advice given. [1]
### Follow-up
- Allergy clinic; general practitioner review; trigger avoidance plan. [1]
### Patient education
- Carry two adrenaline auto-injectors; know the trigger; return at once for any recurrence.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Avoid the confirmed trigger and read ingredient labels.
### Team referrals
- Allergy and immunology; pharmacy for auto-injector training. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Give early IM adrenaline and recognise and treat refractory anaphylaxis.
### Key points
- If in doubt, give IM adrenaline. [2]
- Antihistamines and steroids do not treat airway, breathing or circulation problems. [2]
### Pitfalls
- Delaying adrenaline for antihistamine. [2]
- Sitting the patient up when hypotensive. [2]
- Missing biphasic reactions. [1]
