# Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS)

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and first 24 hours of management of Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS), based on the Joint British Diabetes Societies (JBDS) 2022 guideline, with the Saudi Ministry of Health (MOH) diabetes emergencies protocol and a review of the 2024 international consensus shown side by side where they differ.

## Pathophysiology
HHS is severe hyperglycaemia with marked dehydration and high plasma osmolality but little or no ketosis or acidosis.
- Relative insulin deficiency is enough to cause high glucose but suppresses fat breakdown, so ketone production stays low.
- Very high glucose causes a prolonged osmotic diuresis and a large fluid deficit.
- Rising osmolality draws water out of brain cells, which lowers conscious level.
- Falling glucose or sodium too fast during treatment can shift water into the brain and risks cerebral oedema or central pontine myelinolysis.
- Hyperosmolar blood and dehydration raise the risk of thrombosis. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Days of increasing thirst, passing large volumes of urine and weakness.
- Confusion, drowsiness or reduced responsiveness.
### Examination signs
- Marked dehydration with tachycardia and low blood pressure.
- Reduced conscious level in severe cases.
- Foot pressure damage or ulceration in people who are drowsy or immobile. [1]
### Red flags
- Osmolality above 350 mOsm/kg. [1]
- Sodium above 160 mmol/L. [1]
- Venous or arterial pH below 7.1. [1]
- Potassium below 3.5 or above 6.0 mmol/L on admission. [1]
- Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score below 12 or an abnormal Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive (AVPU) score. [1]
- Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP) below 90 mmHg, oxygen saturation below 92% on air or urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/hour. [1]
- Creatinine above 200 µmol/L and/or Acute Kidney Injury (AKI); hypothermia; myocardial infarction or stroke. [1]
### Precipitants
- Infection, particularly pneumonia or urinary tract infection.
- Acute vascular events such as myocardial infarction or stroke.
- Medicines that raise glucose or cause fluid loss, such as steroids and diuretics.
- Missed or insufficient diabetes treatment, or undiagnosed type 2 diabetes.
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) | Ketones above 3.0 mmol/L with pH below 7.3 or bicarbonate below 15.0 mmol/L |
| Mixed DKA and HHS | Marked hyperosmolality together with pH below 7.3 and ketones above 3.0 mmol/L |
| Sepsis with stress hyperglycaemia | Glucose is high but osmolality and dehydration are not in the HHS range |
| Stroke or hypoglycaemia as a cause of confusion | Focal deficit or low capillary glucose |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Hypovolaemia | Marked hypovolaemia [1] |
| Blood glucose | 30 mmol/L or above (the Saudi protocol says above 30 mmol/L, about 540 mg/dL) [1][2] |
| Osmolality | 320 mOsm/kg or above [1] |
| Ketones | 3.0 mmol/L or below (no significant ketonaemia) [1] |
| Acid–base | Venous pH above 7.3 and bicarbonate above 15.0 mmol/L [1] |
| Effective osmolality (tonicity) | 2 × sodium + glucose, in mmol/L; calculated osmolality adds urea [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–60 minutes)
- Confirm HHS with glucose, osmolality, ketones, pH and bicarbonate before starting; if pH is below 7.3 and ketones are above 3.0 mmol/L, treat as DKA or mixed DKA and HHS. [1]
- Give 0.9% sodium chloride 1 L over the first hour; go faster if SBP is below 90 mmHg. [1]
- Do not start insulin early unless ketones are above 1.0 mmol/L (see Medicines). [1]
- Measure hourly: glucose, sodium, potassium, urea and osmolality for the first 6 hours, and ketones until resolved. [1]
- Check GCS or neurological status every 1–2 hours; keep a fluid balance chart with a minimum urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/hour. [1]
- Protect the feet: assume high risk, off-load heels and check daily. [1]
### Ongoing (1–24 hours)
- From 60 minutes to 6 hours give a further 0.5–1 L/hour of 0.9% sodium chloride, aiming for a positive balance of 2–3 L by 6 hours and 3–6 L by 12 hours. [1]
- Keep sodium change within 10 mmol/L per 24 hours, the fall in osmolality at 3.0–8.0 mOsm/kg per hour and the fall in glucose no more than 5.0 mmol/L per hour; reduce fluid and/or insulin if osmolality falls faster than 8.0 mOsm/kg per hour. [1]
- Use 0.45% sodium chloride only if osmolality is not falling despite adequate 0.9% sodium chloride. [1]
- Use more cautious fluid replacement in people under 50 kg and in heart or renal disease. [1]
- Give prophylactic Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH) for the whole admission unless contraindicated; therapeutic anticoagulation only for suspected thrombosis or acute coronary syndrome. [1]
- After the first 6 hours, if the response is satisfactory, move glucose, sodium, potassium and urea to 2-hourly checks. [1]
- HHS is resolved when osmolality is below 300 mOsm/kg, hypovolaemia is corrected (urine output at least 0.5 mL/kg/hour), cognition is back to pre-morbid state and glucose is below 15 mmol/L. [1]
### Escalation
- Any red-flag marker above (for example osmolality above 350 mOsm/kg, sodium above 160 mmol/L, pH below 7.1, GCS below 12, SBP below 90 mmHg) means HDU or Level 2 care. [1]
- Mixed DKA and HHS: treat using the DKA pathway at 0.1 units/kg/hour. [1]
- Osmolality falling too fast or sodium changing faster than 10 mmol/L per 24 hours: reduce fluid and/or insulin and seek senior advice. [1]

### Discharge criteria
- HHS resolved (osmolality below 300 mOsm/kg, glucose below 15 mmol/L, normal cognition, adequate urine output) and eating and drinking. [1]
- Diabetes education done and a written care plan copied to the General Practitioner (GP). [1]
### Follow-up
- Diabetes Specialist Team (DST) review within one working day and before discharge. [1]
- Follow-up by the diabetes team or primary care after discharge; most people go home on subcutaneous insulin. [1]
### Patient education
- Diabetes education before discharge, including what to do when unwell. [1]
### Lifestyle and diet
- Dietary and glucose-monitoring advice from the diabetes team. [1]
### Team referrals
- DST on admission; HDU or critical care for red-flag markers; podiatry or tissue viability if foot damage is found. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Recognise HHS and separate it from DKA and mixed disease.
- Replace fluid slowly and monitor sodium, osmolality and glucose hourly.
- Know when insulin is started and when to escalate.
### Key points
- HHS is treated mainly with fluid; insulin is started early only if ketones are above 1.0 mmol/L. [1]
- Keep sodium change within 10 mmol/L per 24 hours and glucose fall within 5.0 mmol/L per hour. [1]
- Give LMWH and protect the feet. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Giving a DKA dose of insulin (0.1 units/kg/hour) to a person with HHS. [1]
- Lowering glucose or sodium too fast, which risks cerebral oedema. [1]
- Treating a rising sodium early in treatment as a reason to switch to hypotonic fluid. [1]
- Missing a mixed DKA and HHS picture. [1]
