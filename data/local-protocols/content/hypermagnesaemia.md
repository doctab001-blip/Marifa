# Hypermagnesaemia

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and treatment of hypermagnesaemia, based on the Queensland Health adult electrolyte prescribing guideline and a UK Medicines Information FAQ (Leeds Teaching Hospitals practice, updated 2021). Obstetric magnesium treatment is outside this protocol: contact an obstetrician. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Hypermagnesaemia is almost always caused by reduced kidney excretion combined with a magnesium load. [1] [2]
- Causes: kidney failure, magnesium infusions (pre-eclampsia treatment), magnesium-containing antacids, laxatives, enemas, supplements and lithium. [1]
- High magnesium blocks neuromuscular and cardiac conduction: loss of reflexes, weakness, respiratory depression, hypotension, bradycardia, heart block and coma. [1]
- Calcium antagonises the effect immediately but briefly. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Nausea, flushing, drowsiness, weakness.
### Examination signs
- Loss of deep tendon reflexes, hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, paralysis, reduced consciousness. [1]
### Red flags
- Magnesium above 2.5 mmol/L (severe) with symptoms. [1]
- Loss of reflexes, respiratory depression or heart block. [1]
- Kidney failure. [1] [2]
### Precipitants
- Magnesium sulphate infusions, antacids, enemas, laxatives, supplements, lithium, kidney injury. [1]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Opioid or sedative overdose | Pinpoint pupils or sedative history; magnesium normal |
| Hypocalcaemia | Tetany and hyperreflexia, not loss of reflexes |
| Myasthenic crisis | Fatigable weakness with preserved cardiac signs |
| Hyperkalaemia | Peaked T waves; check potassium |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Normal magnesium | 0.7–1.0 mmol/L |
| Asymptomatic hypermagnesaemia | 2–4 mmol/L [2] |
| Symptomatic or severe | Above 4 mmol/L [2]; Queensland: severe range above 2.5 mmol/L [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Stop all magnesium sources and review medicines (antacids, enemas, supplements, lithium). [1] [2]
- Symptomatic or severe: calcium gluconate IV to protect the heart and breathing, with monitoring. [1] [2]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Promote magnesium excretion with IV 0.9% sodium chloride, aiming for urine output of at least 60 mL/hour. [1]
- Add IV furosemide if fluids alone do not reach this urine output; the dose is not stated in the opened text. [1]
- Recheck magnesium at 4 hours (symptomatic) or 24 hours (asymptomatic). [2]
### Escalation
- Not falling after repeated calcium, or kidney failure: dialysis. [1] [2]
- Respiratory depression: airway support and senior or critical care review.
### Disposition
- Admit symptomatic or severe hypermagnesaemia and kidney failure. [1]

| Parameter | Queensland Health adult guideline [1] | UK Medicines Information FAQ [2] |

| Fluids | 0.9% sodium chloride, urine output at least 60 mL/hour | Maintain good urine output |

### Discharge criteria
- Magnesium normal on repeat, symptoms resolved, source removed and kidney function reviewed. [1] [2]
### Follow-up
- Repeat magnesium within 24 hours and kidney function review. [2]
### Patient education
- Avoid magnesium-containing antacids and laxatives if kidney disease; report weakness or slow breathing.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Review supplements and over-the-counter products with a pharmacist.
### Team referrals
- Nephrology for kidney failure or dialysis; obstetrician if magnesium was given in pregnancy. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Recognise hypermagnesaemia and give calcium to protect the heart and breathing.
- Know when to use fluids, diuretics and dialysis.
### Key points
- Stop the magnesium source first. [1] [2]
- Calcium effect is immediate but short-lived. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Giving magnesium to patients with kidney failure. [2]
- Forgetting the 4-hour recheck after calcium. [2]
- Missing hidden sources such as antacids and enemas. [1]
