# Hypoglycaemia in Adults

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) and inpatient treatment of hypoglycaemia in people with diabetes, based on the Joint British Diabetes Societies (JBDS) 2023 guideline and the Saudi Ministry of Health (MOH) inpatient hypoglycaemia protocol. Neonatal, paediatric and non-diabetic hypoglycaemia work-up are outside this protocol.

## Pathophysiology
Hypoglycaemia in diabetes is usually caused by treatment that lowers glucose faster than intake or hepatic glucose output can match. [1]
- Insulin and sulfonylureas are the usual causes; their effect can last 24–36 hours, longer with renal impairment. [1]
- Missed or delayed meals, extra exercise, alcohol, renal or liver failure and dosing errors are common triggers.
- Low glucose reduces glucose supply to the brain, causing confusion, reduced consciousness and seizures; adrenaline release causes sweating and tremor.

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, anxiety.
- Confusion, odd behaviour, drowsiness, visual disturbance, headache.
### Examination signs
- Pallor, sweating, tachycardia.
- Reduced consciousness, seizure, focal neurological signs that resolve with glucose.
### Red flags
- Unable to swallow safely or unconscious. [1]
- Capillary blood glucose still below 4.0 mmol/L after 3 treatment cycles or 30–45 minutes. [1]
- Sulfonylurea or long-acting insulin overdose, or renal impairment (prolonged effect). [1]
- Seizure or persistent confusion after glucose is corrected.
### Precipitants
- Insulin or sulfonylurea dose too high, missed meal, extra exercise, alcohol, acute kidney injury, liver failure, sepsis.
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Stroke | Focal deficit that does not resolve when glucose is normal |
| Seizure or post-ictal state | Normal capillary glucose |
| Alcohol intoxication | Normal capillary glucose, smell of alcohol |
| Sepsis | Fever, raised inflammatory markers; hypoglycaemia may coexist |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Treat as hypoglycaemia | Capillary blood glucose below 4.0 mmol/L (72 mg/dL) [1] |
| Looming hypoglycaemia | Below 6.0 mmol/L in a person on insulin or a secretagogue, with a falling trend [1] |
| Non-severe | Self-treated by the patient [1] |
| Severe | Needs help from another person [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Confirm capillary glucose below 4.0 mmol/L and check airway, breathing, circulation and consciousness. [1] [2]
- Stop any insulin infusion immediately; restart it once the episode is treated and review the dose. [1]
- Conscious and able to swallow: give oral rapid-acting carbohydrate. [1]
- Unable to swallow safely: give glucose gel, IV glucose or glucagon according to access and glycogen stores. [1]
- Recheck capillary glucose 10–15 minutes after oral treatment (5 minutes after IV dextrose in the Saudi MOH protocol). [1] [2]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Repeat treatment if glucose is still below 4.0 mmol/L, up to 3 times; call for medical help if still low after 30–45 minutes or 3 cycles. [1]
- Once glucose is above 4.0 mmol/L and the patient has recovered, give long-acting carbohydrate. [1]
- Do not omit a due insulin injection; review which dose was active. [1]
### Escalation
- Still below 4.0 mmol/L after 3 cycles: medical review and IV glucose. [1]
- Sulfonylurea or long-acting insulin effect: monitor glucose for at least 24–48 hours. [1]
### Disposition
- Admit for prolonged monitoring if sulfonylurea or long-acting insulin was the cause, in renal impairment, or after severe hypoglycaemia needing IV treatment. [1]

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | JBDS 2023 [1] | Saudi MOH inpatient protocol [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Treatment threshold | Below 4.0 mmol/L | Below 4.0 mmol/L |
| Oral glucose | 15–20 g, recheck at 10–15 minutes, up to 3 treatments | 15 g, recheck at 15 minutes, up to 3 treatments |
| IV glucose | 100 mL 20% glucose or 200 mL 10% glucose over 15 minutes | 25 mL 50% dextrose (2.5–4.0 mmol/L) or 50 mL 50% dextrose (below 2.5 mmol/L) over 1–3 minutes |
| Glucagon | 1 mg IM, once | 1 mg subcutaneous or IM, recheck at 15 minutes |
| Follow-on carbohydrate | 20 g long-acting (40 g after glucagon) | 30 g complex carbohydrate meal once able to eat |
| Recheck after IV treatment | 10–15 minutes | 5 minutes |
### Discharge criteria
- Glucose above 4.0 mmol/L on repeat checks and the patient is eating and alert. [1]
- Cause identified and the diabetes treatment reviewed; longer observation after sulfonylurea or long-acting insulin. [1]
### Follow-up
- Diabetes team review after any severe hypoglycaemia. [1]
### Patient education
- Recognising early symptoms and carrying fast-acting glucose; what to do when meals are missed.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Regular meals with complex carbohydrate and care with alcohol and exercise.
### Team referrals
- Inpatient diabetes team for severe or recurrent hypoglycaemia. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Recognise hypoglycaemia and choose oral, IV or glucagon treatment safely.
- Know the recheck timing and follow-on carbohydrate.
- Know why sulfonylurea and long-acting insulin need prolonged monitoring.
### Key points
- Treat any glucose below 4.0 mmol/L. [1]
- Use IV glucose when access exists and glycogen stores may be depleted. [1]
- Continue glucose monitoring for 24–48 hours after long-acting causes. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Giving glucagon repeatedly. [1]
- Omitting the follow-on carbohydrate. [1]
- Stopping an insulin infusion but forgetting to restart it. [1]
- Treating hyperglycaemic rebound with stat insulin. [1]
