# Suspected Bacterial Meningitis

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and first-hours treatment of suspected bacterial meningitis, based on the NHS Borders antimicrobial guideline "Bacterial meningitis" (version 3.0, reviewed 1 February 2026) with ceftriaxone, aciclovir and renal values from standard UK practice (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) NG240, not opened). Pregnancy, neurosurgical meningitis, tuberculous meningitis and children are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Bacterial meningitis is infection of the meninges, most often by Streptococcus pneumoniae or Neisseria meningitidis, with Listeria monocytogenes in older and immunocompromised patients. [1]
- Bacteria cross the blood-brain barrier and cause inflammation, raised intracranial pressure and cerebral oedema. [1]
- Meningococcal disease can progress to septicaemia and purpura within hours. [1]
- Delay in antibiotics worsens outcome, so treatment starts before lumbar puncture if it would delay care. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Fever, severe headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, confusion, rash, seizures. [1]
### Examination signs
- Neck stiffness, Kernig and Brudzinski signs, reduced consciousness, non-blanching purpuric rash, focal deficit, papilloedema. [1]
### Red flags
- Non-blanching rash (meningococcal septicaemia). [1]
- Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 12 or below, seizures or focal deficit. [1]
- Shock or signs of raised intracranial pressure. [1]
- Immunocompromise or age 60 or over (Listeria risk). [1]
### Precipitants
- Recent upper respiratory infection, close contact, asplenia, immunosuppression, head injury or neurosurgery, ear or sinus infection. [1]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Viral meningitis | Milder illness; cerebrospinal fluid lymphocytes, normal glucose |
| Viral encephalitis | Behaviour change, seizures; herpes simplex polymerase chain reaction positive |
| Subarachnoid haemorrhage | Thunderclap onset; blood on computed tomography (CT) |
| Sepsis from other source | Source evident and no meningism |
| Migraine | Recurrent, no fever |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Suspected bacterial meningitis | Fever with headache, neck stiffness or non-blanching rash [1] |
| Bacterial CSF pattern | Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) neutrophils raised, protein raised, glucose below 50% of blood [1] |
### Bedside and bloods
| Test | Normal (adult) | In this condition | What it means / next step | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood cultures | No growth | Positive | Take before antibiotics without delaying them | [1] |
| Capillary and venous glucose | 4–7 mmol/L | Used for CSF glucose ratio | Compare with CSF | [1] |
| Full blood count, C-reactive protein, lactate, clotting | Normal | Raised inflammatory markers; coagulopathy | Severity | [1] |
| Meningococcal and pneumococcal polymerase chain reaction on blood | Negative | Positive | Confirms organism | [1] |
| Lumbar puncture CSF (cells, protein, glucose, Gram stain, culture, polymerase chain reaction) | Cells below 5/µL | Neutrophils raised | Confirms bacterial meningitis | [1] |
### Imaging and specialist
| Test | Normal (adult) | In this condition | What it means / next step | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CT head before lumbar puncture | No mass effect | Mass effect or midline shift | Do not do lumbar puncture; treat first | [1] |
| Microbiology and infectious diseases advice | Not needed | Immunocompromised, post-neurosurgery or uncertain cause | Consultant microbiologist | [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Airway, breathing and circulation; blood cultures; give antibiotics at once without waiting for CT or lumbar puncture. [1]
- Give dexamethasone ideally just before or with the first antibiotic dose. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Add amoxicillin if age 60 or over, immunocompromised or Listeria suspected. [1]
- Fluid resuscitation and critical care for shock; CT head if criteria for delay of lumbar puncture. [1]
### Escalation
- Reduced consciousness, shock or raised intracranial pressure: critical care. [1]
### Disposition
- Admit all; notifiable disease reported; contact prophylaxis for close contacts per public health. [1]

| Parameter | NHS Borders v3.0 (2026) [1] | NICE NG240 standard practice (not opened) [3] |
|---|---|---|
| Empirical antibiotic | Cefotaxime 2 g every 6 hours | Ceftriaxone 2 g every 12 hours |
| Listeria cover | Amoxicillin 2 g every 4 hours, age 60 or over or immunocompromised | Amoxicillin if age 60 or over |
| Dexamethasone | 10 mg every 6 hours for 4 days, consider | 10 mg every 6 hours for 4 days |
| Allergy | Chloramphenicol 25 mg/kg (maximum 2 g) every 6 hours | Per microbiology |
| Duration | Meningococcal 7 days; pneumococcal 14 days | Same |
### Discharge criteria
- Not for ED discharge; completion of IV course and reviewed by infectious diseases. [1]
### Follow-up
- Hearing assessment; infectious diseases review; public health contact tracing. [1]
### Patient education
- Complete the course; report headache, deafness or rash; household contacts need prophylaxis.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Keep vaccinations up to date, including meningococcal and pneumococcal.
### Team referrals
- Infectious diseases; critical care; audiology; public health. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Start treatment without delay and select antibiotics by age and allergy.
### Key points
- Antibiotics before CT or lumbar puncture if delay is likely. [1]
- Dexamethasone with or just before the first antibiotic dose. [1]
- Notifiable disease. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Waiting for lumbar puncture. [1]
- Forgetting Listeria cover in older patients. [1]
- Missing the rash. [1]
