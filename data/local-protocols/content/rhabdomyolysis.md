# Rhabdomyolysis

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and first-hours management of acute rhabdomyolysis, based on the Scottish Managed Network "Management of Acute Rhabdomyolysis in Adults" (NSD610-018.29 V1) and the Danish Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine (DASAIM) guideline (2019). Exertional rhabdomyolysis in athletes, children, pregnancy and established dialysis patients are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Rhabdomyolysis is breakdown of skeletal muscle that releases myoglobin, potassium, phosphate and creatine kinase (CK) into the blood. [1]
- Causes: crush injury, prolonged immobility, seizures, exertion, heat illness, statins, drugs and toxins, infection. [1]
- Myoglobin injures the kidney tubules and causes acute kidney injury (AKI). [1] [2]
- Potassium release causes life-threatening hyperkalaemia; calcium falls early and rises later. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Muscle pain, weakness and swelling; dark (tea-coloured) urine. [1]
### Examination signs
- Tender swollen muscles, tense compartments, low urine output, signs of dehydration. [1]
### Red flags
- Potassium 6.5 mmol/L or above or electrocardiogram (ECG) changes. [1]
- Oliguria or anuria. [1]
- Compartment syndrome (pain on passive stretch, tense compartment). [1]
- CK above 15 000 IU/L (high risk of AKI). [1]
### Precipitants
- Crush injury, long lie, seizures, strenuous exercise, statins, alcohol or drug use, heat. [1]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Myocardial injury | Troponin raised with normal or mildly raised CK |
| Haematuria | Red blood cells on urine microscopy |
| Acute kidney injury from other cause | CK below 1000 IU/L |
| Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | Fever, rigidity after antipsychotic use |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Rhabdomyolysis | CK above 1000 IU/L (about five times the upper limit of normal) with a compatible history [1] p. 2 |
| Urine | Dipstick positive for blood with no red cells on microscopy [1] p. 2 |
### Bedside and bloods
| Test | Normal (adult) | In this condition | What it means / next step | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Creatine kinase | Below 200 IU/L | Above 1000 IU/L | Start fluids; repeat every 6–12 hours | [1] p. 2 |
| Potassium | 3.5–5.0 mmol/L | Raised | 6.5 mmol/L or above: treat as emergency | [1] p. 3 |
| Calcium, phosphate | Calcium 2.2–2.6 mmol/L | Calcium low | Treat only if symptomatic or hyperkalaemia | [1] p. 3 |
| Urea, creatinine | Creatinine 60–110 µmol/L | Raised | Stage AKI | [1] p. 2 |
| Venous blood gas | pH 7.35–7.45 | Acidosis | Seek renal advice if pH below 7.2 | [1] p. 3 |
| Urine pH | 5–7 | Below 6.5 | Target above 6.5 if bicarbonate used | [1] p. 4 |
| 12-lead ECG | Normal | Peaked T waves, wide QRS | Treat hyperkalaemia | [1] p. 3 |
### Imaging and specialist
| Test | Normal (adult) | In this condition | What it means / next step | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Compartment pressure and surgical review | Soft compartments | Tense painful compartment | Urgent orthopaedic review for fasciotomy | [1] p. 4 |
| Nephrology advice | Not needed | Refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis, fluid overload or oliguria | Discuss renal replacement therapy | [1] p. 4 |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Start intravenous (IV) crystalloid at 1.5 L/hour (see Medicines). [1] p. 3
- Treat hyperkalaemia with ECG changes. [1] p. 3
- Insert a urinary catheter and measure hourly urine output. [1] p. 3
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Aim for urine output 200–300 mL/hour; continue fluids until CK is below 1000 IU/L and urine is clear. [1] p. 3
- Repeat CK, potassium, creatinine and calcium every 6–12 hours. [1] p. 3
- Avoid mannitol and routine loop diuretics. [1] p. 4; [2]
### Escalation
- No urine response, refractory hyperkalaemia or acidosis, or fluid overload: nephrology for dialysis. [1] p. 4
### Disposition
- Admit all with CK above 1000 IU/L; high dependency if CK above 15 000 IU/L, potassium raised or AKI. [1] p. 2

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | SMN NSD610-018.29 [1] | DASAIM 2019 [2] |
|---|---|---|
| CK threshold | Above 1000 IU/L | Above 5 times upper limit of normal |
| Fluid | Crystalloid 1.5 L/hour; urine 200–300 mL/hour | Suggests crystalloid |
| Bicarbonate | Optional; urine pH above 6.5 | Suggests against routine use |
| Mannitol and diuretics | Avoid mannitol | Against routine mannitol and loop diuretics |
| Renal replacement | Refractory hyperkalaemia or overload | Against routine early use |
### Discharge criteria
- CK below 1000 IU/L and falling, normal potassium, creatinine stable or falling, passing clear urine and cause removed. [1] p. 4
### Follow-up
- Creatinine, potassium and CK within 48–72 hours; review of statins and causative drugs. [1] p. 4
### Patient education
- Drink fluids freely; return for dark urine, muscle pain or reduced urine.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Build up exercise gradually; avoid dehydration and heavy alcohol.
### Team referrals
- Nephrology; orthopaedics for compartment syndrome; toxicology for drug causes. [1] p. 4
### Learning objectives
- Recognise rhabdomyolysis, start aggressive fluid and treat hyperkalaemia and compartment syndrome.
### Key points
- Early high-volume fluid protects the kidney. [1] p. 3
- Check potassium on arrival and repeat. [1] p. 3
- Do not give mannitol. [1] p. 4
### Pitfalls
- Giving calcium for asymptomatic low calcium. [1] p. 3
- Missing compartment syndrome. [1] p. 4
- Fluid overload in older patients. [1] p. 3
