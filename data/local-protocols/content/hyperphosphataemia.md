# Hyperphosphataemia

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and first treatment of hyperphosphataemia, mostly in kidney failure, based on the Derbyshire Joint Area Prescribing Committee (JAPC) phosphate binder information sheet and the Barts Health and North East London shared care guideline for phosphate binders. The opened sources cover phosphate binders and targets in chronic kidney disease; they do not give an emergency intravenous regimen, so acute severe cases need senior or nephrology advice. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Hyperphosphataemia is a raised serum phosphate, usually because the kidney cannot excrete phosphate. [1]
- Causes: chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, tumour lysis, rhabdomyolysis, phosphate-containing enemas or laxatives, hypoparathyroidism and vitamin D excess.
- High phosphate binds calcium and lowers calcium, which can cause tetany, and over time causes calcification and cardiovascular harm. [1]
- Dietary phosphate is absorbed, so binders are used with diet and dialysis. [1] [2]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Usually none; itch, bone pain or muscle cramps in chronic kidney disease.
- Tingling and cramps if calcium is low.
### Examination signs
- Signs of kidney failure; tetany if calcium is low.
### Red flags
- Acute kidney injury, tumour lysis or rhabdomyolysis with rising phosphate.
- Low calcium with electrocardiogram (ECG) changes.
- Phosphate-containing enema or laxative overuse.
### Precipitants
- Kidney failure, tumour lysis, rhabdomyolysis, phosphate enemas, vitamin D excess.
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Pseudohyperphosphataemia | Paraprotein or haemolysed sample; resample |
| Tumour lysis syndrome | Recent chemotherapy; high potassium and uric acid |
| Hypoparathyroidism | Low calcium with low parathyroid hormone |
| Chronic kidney disease | Long-standing raised creatinine |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Normal serum phosphate | 0.8–1.5 mmol/L (reference) |
| Target in chronic kidney disease stage 4 or 5, not on dialysis | 0.9–1.5 mmol/L [2] |
| Target on dialysis | 1.1–1.7 mmol/L [2] |
| Licensed binder use in non-dialysis kidney disease | Phosphate 1.78 mmol/L or above [2] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Check calcium, potassium, creatinine and ECG; treat life-threatening hyperkalaemia or hypocalcaemia first. [1]
- Stop phosphate-containing enemas, laxatives and supplements.
- Severe or acute rise (tumour lysis, acute kidney injury): urgent senior or nephrology advice; dialysis reduces phosphate. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Choose a binder by corrected calcium (see Medicines); take with meals. [1]
- Dietary phosphate restriction with renal dietitian assessment. [1]
### Escalation
- Rising phosphate with falling calcium or acute kidney injury: nephrology urgently. [1]
### Disposition
- Admit if acute kidney injury, tumour lysis, low calcium with symptoms or ECG change. Stable chronic patients can continue binders as outpatients with follow-up. [1]

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | Derbyshire JAPC binder sheet [1] | Barts Health and North East London shared care guideline [2] |
|---|---|---|
| First-line binder | Calcium acetate when calcium is normal | Calcium-based binders first; sevelamer if calcium raised |
| Sevelamer | 2.4–4.8 g daily in three divided doses | 800–1600 mg three times daily with meals; usual 6 g daily |
| Lanthanum | 1.5–3 g daily in divided doses | Start 750 mg daily; maximum 3750 mg daily |
| Targets | None stated | 0.9–1.5 mmol/L (not on dialysis); 1.1–1.7 (dialysis) |
| Monitoring | Phosphate, calcium and parathyroid hormone at least quarterly | Phosphate and calcium every 2–4 weeks |
### Discharge criteria
- Phosphate falling toward target, calcium stable, a binder plan and renal follow-up arranged. [1] [2]
### Follow-up
- Phosphate and corrected calcium every 2–4 weeks initially; liver function quarterly on lanthanum. [2]
### Patient education
- Take binders with meals; follow low phosphate diet advice.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Renal dietitian assessment before binders and dietary phosphate restriction. [1]
### Team referrals
- Nephrology and renal dietitian. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Choose a binder by calcium level and take it with meals.
- Recognise acute causes needing urgent nephrology input.
### Key points
- Titrate binders to phosphate and watch calcium. [1] [2]
- Calcium salts are contraindicated in hypercalcaemia. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Using calcium binders in hypercalcaemia. [1]
- Crushing calcium acetate tablets. [1]
- Missing an acute cause such as tumour lysis. [1]
