# Hypophosphataemia

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and level-based replacement of acute hypophosphataemia, based on the Worcestershire Acute Hospitals NHS Trust guideline (version 6, January 2026) and the UK Medicines Information (UKMi) Medicines Q&A (2017). Refeeding syndrome, chronic hypophosphataemia and significant renal impairment are managed on local refeeding or specialist pathways and are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Hypophosphataemia is a serum phosphate below 0.8 mmol/L. [1]
- Causes: refeeding, alcohol use disorder, diabetic ketoacidosis treatment with insulin, respiratory alkalosis, malabsorption, vitamin D deficiency, phosphate-binding antacids, burns and renal losses.
- Phosphate moves into cells with insulin and glucose, so levels fall quickly during treatment of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and refeeding.
- Severe depletion reduces cellular energy: muscle weakness, respiratory failure, heart failure, arrhythmia, seizures, haemolysis and rhabdomyolysis.
- Phosphate is cleared by the kidney, so replacement can cause hyperphosphataemia in renal impairment. [2]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Often none in mild disease. [1]
- Weakness, fatigue, bone pain, confusion; symptoms usually appear below 0.3 mmol/L. [2]
### Examination signs
- Proximal muscle weakness, reduced reflexes, respiratory muscle weakness, confusion or seizure.
### Red flags
- Phosphate below 0.32 mmol/L (severe). [1]
- Respiratory failure, heart failure, arrhythmia, seizure or rhabdomyolysis.
- Refeeding risk (long starvation, alcohol use, eating disorder).
- Renal impairment (high risk of over-replacement). [1] [2]
### Precipitants
- Insulin treatment in DKA, refeeding, alcohol withdrawal, binders, sepsis, malabsorption.
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Refeeding syndrome | Recent restart of feeding after starvation; low potassium and magnesium too |
| Hypomagnesaemia | Low magnesium; may coexist |
| Hyperventilation | Respiratory alkalosis on blood gas; resolves with breathing |
| Hyperparathyroidism | High calcium with low phosphate |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Normal serum phosphate | 0.8–1.5 mmol/L [1] |
| Mild | 0.6–0.79 mmol/L; usually no treatment if asymptomatic [1] |
| Moderate | 0.32–0.59 mmol/L (UKMi: 0.3–0.6 mmol/L) [1] [2] |
| Severe | Below 0.32 mmol/L (UKMi: below 0.3 mmol/L) [1] [2] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Grade by serum phosphate and check symptoms, calcium, potassium, magnesium and renal function. [1] [2]
- Prefer the enteral route where possible; use IV for severe or symptomatic cases, or when oral is not absorbed or tolerated. [1] [2]
- Correct hypocalcaemia before replacing phosphate. [2]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Severe: phosphate, calcium, potassium and magnesium every 12 hours; moderate: same electrolytes daily until phosphate is back in range; renal impairment: phosphate daily. [1]
- Stop replacement once phosphate is 0.8–1.5 mmol/L. [1]
- Patients under 60 kg: use half the table dose (Worcestershire). [1]
- Preparation: Phosphates Polyfusor 500 mL contains 50 mmol phosphate, 9.5 mmol potassium and 81 mmol sodium; give each phosphate infusion through its own line and do not mix with other injections. [1] [2]
### Escalation
- Arrhythmia, respiratory failure or severe hypocalcaemia during infusion: stop and call for senior or critical care review. [1]
- Renal impairment or dialysis: specialist advice before IV replacement. [2]
### Disposition
- Admit severe or symptomatic hypophosphataemia, and any IV replacement. [1]

### Discharge criteria
- Phosphate 0.8 mmol/L or above on repeat testing, eating, no arrhythmia and the cause treated. [1]
### Follow-up
- Repeat phosphate, calcium, potassium and magnesium within days if on oral replacement; refeeding follow-up per local guideline. [1]
### Patient education
- Take effervescent tablets dissolved in water and apart from calcium or magnesium supplements.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Dietary phosphate advice from a dietitian; reduce alcohol.
### Team referrals
- Dietitian or refeeding team; nephrology in renal impairment. [1] [2]
### Learning objectives
- Grade hypophosphataemia, give oral or IV phosphate by level and weight, and monitor safely.
### Key points
- Use half the table dose if under 60 kg (Worcestershire). [1]
- Correct low calcium first and check electrolytes every 12 hours in severe disease. [1] [2]
### Pitfalls
- Over-replacement in renal impairment. [1] [2]
- Giving oral phosphate with calcium or magnesium salts. [1] [2]
- Mixing other injections with phosphate. [1]
