# Acute Severe Asthma in Adults

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) assessment and treatment of acute asthma, including acute severe and life-threatening attacks, based on the Right Decisions ED asthma algorithm that implements the British Thoracic Society (BTS) and Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) guideline 158 (2019), with aminophylline dosing from the UK summary of product characteristics. Pregnancy, children and chronic asthma step-up are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Acute asthma is airway narrowing from smooth muscle spasm, mucus plugging and inflammation, causing wheeze and breathlessness. [1] [2]
- Triggers include viral infection, allergens, smoke, pollution, medicines (aspirin, beta-blockers) and poor adherence. [2]
- Severe attacks cause air trapping, hypoxia and tiring, which leads to hypercapnia and respiratory arrest. [1]
- A normal or rising carbon dioxide in an attack is an ominous sign. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Breathlessness, wheeze, chest tightness, cough, inability to complete sentences. [1]
### Examination signs
- Tachypnoea, wheeze, accessory muscle use, tachycardia; silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion or confusion in life-threatening asthma. [1]
### Red flags
- Peak expiratory flow (PEF) below 33% of best or predicted. [1]
- Silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, arrhythmia, exhaustion or altered consciousness. [1]
- Normal or raised arterial carbon dioxide above 4.6 kPa. [1]
- Arterial oxygen below 8 kPa or low pH. [1]
### Precipitants
- Respiratory infection, allergen exposure, non-adherence to inhaled steroid, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, beta-blockers. [2]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation | Smoking history, age over 40, chronic breathlessness |
| Acute heart failure | Crackles, raised jugular venous pressure and oedema |
| Pulmonary embolism | Pleuritic pain, no wheeze, raised D-dimer |
| Anaphylaxis | Skin changes, angioedema, hypotension |
| Upper airway obstruction | Stridor, not wheeze |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Moderate acute asthma | PEF above 50–75% of best or predicted [1] |
| Acute severe asthma | PEF 33–50% [1] |
| Life-threatening asthma | PEF below 33% [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Oxygen to keep saturation 94–98%; do not delay if oximetry is unavailable. [1] [2] p. 8
- Nebulised salbutamol with ipratropium, oxygen-driven; give steroid at once. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Repeat salbutamol with ipratropium after 15 minutes; consider continuous salbutamol. [1]
- Consider IV magnesium sulfate for acute severe or life-threatening asthma. [1]
- Measure PEF at 15–20 minutes, 60 minutes and 120 minutes. [1]
### Escalation
- Any life-threatening feature or no improvement: senior and critical care immediately. [1]
### Disposition
- Discharge if stable with PEF above 75%; admit if PEF below 50% on presentation or not improving. [1]

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | Right Decisions ED algorithm (BTS/SIGN 158) [1] | SIGN 158 section 2.7 [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Severity | PEF >50–75% moderate; 33–50% acute severe; below 33% life-threatening | Refer acute severe or life-threatening to hospital |
| Oxygen | SpO2 94–98% | Controlled oxygen titrated to 94–98% |
| Bronchodilator | Salbutamol 5 mg plus ipratropium 0.5 mg nebulised | High-dose inhaled beta-agonists first-line |
| Steroid | Prednisolone 40–50 mg or hydrocortisone 100 mg | Adequate-dose steroids for all |
| Magnesium | 1.2–2 g over 20 minutes | Not detailed in section read |
### Discharge criteria
- Stable, PEF above 75% of best or predicted, no extra observation need, inhaled treatment supply and technique checked. [1]
### Follow-up
- General practitioner review within 2 working days; asthma nurse or chest clinic referral. [1]
### Patient education
- Use the inhaler with a spacer; follow the written action plan; return if PEF falls or breathlessness returns.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Avoid known triggers and smoke; keep vaccinations up to date.
### Team referrals
- Asthma liaison nurse; respiratory clinic. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Grade acute asthma, treat with oxygen, nebulisers and steroid, and recognise life-threatening features.
### Key points
- Give steroid early in every attack. [1] [2]
- Normal carbon dioxide in a severe attack means the patient is tiring. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Sedating a breathless asthmatic. [1]
- Missing hypokalaemia after repeated salbutamol. [1]
- Discharging without a steroid course. [1]
