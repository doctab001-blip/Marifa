# Hyperkalaemia

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and treatment of acute hyperkalaemia, including hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest, based on the UK Kidney Association (UKKA) 2023 guideline and the 2025 European Resuscitation Council (ERC) special circumstances guideline. No Saudi national hyperkalaemia guideline was found; this protocol adopts the UKKA and ERC recommendations as the Saudi reference.

## Pathophysiology
Hyperkalaemia is a serum potassium of 5.5 mmol/L or above. It is dangerous because high extracellular potassium disturbs the electrical activity of the heart. [1]
- Potassium excretion falls in Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), chronic kidney disease and with drugs that block the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.
- Potassium moves out of cells in acidosis, insulin deficiency, tissue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, crush injury) and with some drugs.
- A high extracellular potassium raises the resting membrane potential and slows conduction, which produces peaked T waves, flattened P waves, a wide QRS complex and arrhythmias. [1]
- Treatment has three aims: protect the heart, shift potassium into cells, and remove potassium from the body. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Often none; found on routine or acute blood tests.
- Muscle weakness, palpitations or light-headedness.
### Examination signs
- Slow or irregular pulse.
- Reduced muscle power in severe cases.
### Red flags
- Potassium 6.5 mmol/L or above (severe). [1]
- Electrocardiogram (ECG) features of hyperkalaemia. [1]
- Potassium 6.0–6.4 mmol/L in a patient who is unwell or whose potassium is rising rapidly. [1]
- Arrhythmia, collapse or cardiac arrest. [1]
- Oliguria or AKI with rising potassium.
### Precipitants
- AKI or chronic kidney disease.
- Medicines: renin–angiotensin system blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, potassium supplements and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.
- Tissue breakdown: rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis and burns.
- Diabetic ketoacidosis and other acidosis.
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Pseudohyperkalaemia | Haemolysed or delayed sample with a normal ECG; repeat the test |
| Hyperkalaemia from AKI | Raised creatinine with low urine output |
| Hyperkalaemia from tissue breakdown | Very high creatine kinase, or known chemotherapy for tumour lysis |
| Hyperkalaemia in diabetic ketoacidosis | Ketones above 3.0 mmol/L with acidosis |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Get a 12-lead ECG for every hospital patient with potassium 6.0 mmol/L or above and start cardiac monitoring when indicated. [1]
- Use the five-step approach and never walk away without completing every step. [1]
- Step 1, protect the heart: for ECG changes give intravenous calcium (see Medicines); give it even if dialysis is immediately available. [1]
- Step 2, shift potassium into cells: insulin with glucose, with nebulised salbutamol as an adjunct (never alone). [1]
- Step 3, remove potassium: give sodium zirconium cyclosilicate for severe hyperkalaemia; calcium resonium is no longer routinely recommended. [1]
- Check blood glucose before and after insulin; give 10% glucose at 50 mL/hour for 5 hours if the pre-treatment glucose is below 7.0 mmol/L. [1]
- Do not use intravenous sodium bicarbonate for routine acute treatment. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Monitor glucose at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300 and 360 minutes after insulin. [1]
- Repeat potassium at least 1, 2, 4, 6 and 24 hours after moderate or severe hyperkalaemia is treated, and watch for rebound. [1]
- Find and treat the cause (AKI, medicines, tissue breakdown, acidosis) and stop medicines that raise potassium. [1]
### Escalation
- Potassium 6.5 mmol/L or above, or toxic ECG changes: urgent dialysis is recommended; give calcium without waiting. [1]
- Dialysis not immediately available: continue standard medical therapy. [1]
- Cardiac arrest: follow the Advanced Life Support (ALS) pathway and give calcium chloride and sodium bicarbonate (see the cardiac arrest branch). [1] [2]
### Disposition
- Admit moderate or severe hyperkalaemia for monitoring and repeat potassium; severe hyperkalaemia needs immediate admission. [1]
- Mild hyperkalaemia in a well patient: repeat the test within 3 days, and within 1 day for moderate. [1]
### Medicines
Level-based treatment: mild 5.5–5.9 mmol/L needs no acute drug (find the cause); moderate 6.0–6.4 mmol/L use the first branch; severe 6.5 mmol/L or above, or any ECG changes, use the second branch. Give calcium first whenever the ECG is abnormal.

### Discharge criteria
- Potassium back in the normal range on repeat testing, a normal ECG and the cause found and addressed. [1]
- Medicines that raise potassium reviewed before discharge.
### Follow-up
- Repeat potassium within 3 days for mild, within 1 day for moderate; severe hyperkalaemia needs immediate admission. [1]
- Kidney or medicine review for recurrent hyperkalaemia, including whether a long-term potassium binder is needed. [1]
### Patient education
- Which medicines and foods can raise potassium, and why the repeat blood test matters.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Dietary potassium advice from a dietitian for people with kidney disease.
### Team referrals
- Nephrology or critical care urgently for severe hyperkalaemia or dialysis decisions. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Grade hyperkalaemia and recognise ECG changes.
- Give calcium, an insulin and glucose infusion and a potassium binder in the right order.
- Know when to call for dialysis and what to give in cardiac arrest.
### Key points
- Protect the heart first with calcium when the ECG is abnormal. [1]
- Insulin and glucose need glucose monitoring for 6 hours. [1]
- Never leave until every step of the five-step approach is done. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Using salbutamol alone to treat severe hyperkalaemia. [1]
- Forgetting glucose monitoring and hypoglycaemia after insulin. [1]
- Using intravenous bicarbonate as routine acute treatment. [1]
- Using calcium resonium as a routine acute treatment. [1]
