# Hypernatraemia

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and fluid correction of hypernatraemia, based on the NHS Highland adult guideline and a hospital trust management pathway. Diabetes insipidus work-up and paediatric care are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Hypernatraemia is a serum sodium above 145 mmol/L and nearly always means a water deficit. [1]
- Causes: extra-renal water loss (gastrointestinal, fever, sweating, hyperventilation), impaired thirst, renal water loss (polyuria above 3 L in 24 hours) and iatrogenic sodium loading. [1]
- Water moves out of brain cells, causing confusion, irritability, seizures and coma.
- Too rapid correction causes cerebral oedema, so the fall in sodium is limited. [1] [2]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Thirst, weakness, irritability, confusion, drowsiness.
### Examination signs
- Dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, tachycardia, hypotension if hypovolaemic.
- Neurological signs, seizures in severe disease.
### Red flags
- Sodium 160 mmol/L or above (severe). [1]
- Hypotension or shock. [1]
- Neurological signs at presentation or during treatment. [1]
- Glucose above 30 mmol/L with no ketones (suspect hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state). [2]
### Precipitants
- Poor oral intake, vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, diuretics, osmotic diuresis, diabetes insipidus (head injury, lithium), hypertonic saline or sodium bicarbonate. [1] [2]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state | Glucose above 30 mmol/L, no ketones, osmolality above 320 mOsm/kg [2] |
| Diabetes insipidus | Polyuria with dilute urine; poor response to IV hydration [2] |
| Sample contamination | Drawn from an arm with a 0.9% sodium chloride drip [2] |
| Osmotic diuresis | High urine osmolality with polyuria [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Assess airway, breathing, circulation, consciousness and volume status. [1]
- Hypotensive: restore circulating volume first with Plasmalyte, then use hypotonic fluid. [1]
- Mild or moderate: give water orally or enterally. [1]
- Severe: give intravenous 5% dextrose (see Medicines). [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Lower sodium by 0.5–1 mmol/L per hour; do not fall more than 12 mmol/L in the first 24 hours; complete correction over 36–72 hours. [1]
- Check sodium every 4 hours until the target rate is reached; then every 12–24 hours until in range (trust pathway). [1] [2]
- Treat the cause (for example fever or gastrointestinal losses). [1] [2]
- Estimate total body water as 0.6 × weight in kg (men) or 0.5 × weight in kg (women); NHS Highland refers to an online free water deficit calculator because its formula is only an image. [1]
- Correction arithmetic: a fall of 0.5 mmol/L per hour is 12 mmol/L in 24 hours, the upper limit; do not aim to correct faster. [1] [2]
### Escalation
- Neurological signs, rapid fall in sodium, persistent or worsening hypernatraemia, or no enteral or IV route: escalate. [1]
- Suspected diabetes insipidus: renal or endocrine advice. [1]
### Disposition
- Admit severe, symptomatic, hypotensive or unable-to-drink patients. [1]

### Discharge criteria
- Sodium in range or falling safely, drinking independently and cause treated. [1]
### Follow-up
- Repeat sodium within days; follow up the cause. [1]
### Patient education
- Drink enough water, especially in hot weather, illness and old age.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Adequate fluid intake and review of diuretics and other medicines.
### Team referrals
- Renal or endocrine team for diabetes insipidus or severe cases. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Grade hypernatraemia, choose oral or IV water and avoid over-rapid correction.
### Key points
- Correct slowly: no more than 12 mmol/L in 24 hours. [1] [2]
- Resuscitate hypotension first. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Falling sodium too quickly, risking cerebral oedema. [1] [2]
- Missing hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state. [2]
- Sampling from a drip arm. [2]
