# Opioid Overdose

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and treatment of opioid toxicity with respiratory depression using naloxone, based on the University Hospitals Sussex NHS Foundation Trust Guideline for the Use of Naloxone in Adults (version 3.0, June 2023) and the NHS Tayside ED naloxone infusion guidance (reviewed March 2020). Bolus doses follow standard UK practice because neither source gives them. Children, palliative care titration and chronic opioid withdrawal management are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Opioids depress the brainstem respiratory drive, causing slow breathing, hypoxia and hypercapnia. [1]
- Naloxone is a short-acting opioid antagonist (half-life shorter than most opioids), so toxicity can return after a single dose. [1]
- Long-acting opioids (methadone, modified-release preparations, patches) and buprenorphine may need an infusion. [1] [2]
- Rapid reversal in opioid-dependent patients can cause withdrawal, pain, hypertension, arrhythmia or pulmonary oedema. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Drowsiness progressing to unresponsiveness; history of opioid use or overdose from a witness. [1]
### Examination signs
- Respiratory rate below 8 breaths/minute, small pinpoint pupils, reduced consciousness, needle marks or patches. [1]
### Red flags
- Respiratory rate below 8 breaths/minute with reduced alertness. [1]
- Cyanosis or oxygen saturation below 90%. [1]
- Methadone, buprenorphine or modified-release opioids (prolonged toxicity). [1] [2]
- Co-ingestion of alcohol, benzodiazepines or paracetamol. [2]
### Precipitants
- Intentional overdose, accidental excess, drug interaction, renal impairment (accumulation), loss of tolerance after abstinence. [1]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Benzodiazepine overdose | Normal pupils; no response to naloxone |
| Hypoglycaemia | Low capillary glucose |
| Stroke or head injury | Focal deficit, asymmetry |
| Sepsis or encephalopathy | Fever; no response to naloxone |
| Alcohol intoxication | Smell of alcohol, no pinpoint pupils |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Opioid respiratory depression | Respiratory rate below 8 breaths/minute with reduced alertness (P or U on AVPU) or sedation score 2 or more [1] |
| Naloxone not indicated | Slight sedation or opioid-induced delirium [1] |
### Bedside and bloods
| Test | Normal (adult) | In this condition | What it means / next step | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory rate, saturation, sedation score | Rate 12–20; saturation 94–98% | Rate below 8 | Treat with airway support, oxygen and naloxone | [1] |
| Capillary glucose | 4–7 mmol/L | Low | Treat hypoglycaemia | [1] |
| Venous blood gas | pH 7.35–7.45; pCO2 4.6–6.1 kPa | Respiratory acidosis | Support ventilation | [2] |
| Paracetamol and salicylate levels, electrocardiogram (ECG) | Not detected; normal rhythm | Co-ingestion, QT prolongation | Treat co-ingestant | [2] |
| Creatinine | 60–110 µmol/L | Raised | Accumulation of morphine | [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Airway manoeuvres, bag-valve-mask ventilation, oxygen; this treats most hypoxia before naloxone acts. [1]
- Intravenous (IV) access and naloxone titrated to breathing, not to full consciousness. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Observe at least every 15 minutes for up to 2 hours after naloxone; longer for long-acting opioids. [1]
- Start an infusion if repeated doses are needed. [1] [2]
### Escalation
- No response to 2 mg of naloxone: reconsider diagnosis; critical care for ventilation. [1]
### Disposition
- Discharge only when toxicity has resolved and ventilation is adequate without naloxone for at least 6 hours (12 hours for patches or modified-release preparations; methadone 12 hours from ingestion). [2]

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | UHSussex v3.0 (2023) [1] | NHS Tayside ED guidance (2020) [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Bolus dose | Not given; lower in dependent patients | Not given |
| Infusion | 200 micrograms/mL; 10 mg in 50 mL; start 60% of effective bolus per hour | 10 mg to 50 mL (200 micrograms/mL); start about 60% of the total dose needed |
| Observation | At least every 15 minutes for up to 2 hours; longer for long-acting opioids | At least 6 hours (12 hours modified-release or patch) without naloxone; methadone 12 hours |
### Discharge criteria
- Toxicity resolved, ventilation adequate without naloxone for at least 6 hours (12 hours for patches or modified-release), mental health and safeguarding assessed. [2]
### Follow-up
- Addiction service referral; take-home naloxone supply. [1]
### Patient education
- Never use alone; carry naloxone; avoid mixing opioids with alcohol or sedatives.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Engage with drug and alcohol services.
### Team referrals
- Addiction services; liaison psychiatry; poisons service. [2]
### Learning objectives
- Titrate naloxone to breathing and manage long-acting opioid toxicity.
### Key points
- Oxygen and ventilation first; naloxone to respiratory rate. [1]
- Observe for return of toxicity. [2]
### Pitfalls
- Over-reversing a dependent patient. [1]
- Discharging too early after methadone. [2]
- Missing co-ingested paracetamol. [2]
