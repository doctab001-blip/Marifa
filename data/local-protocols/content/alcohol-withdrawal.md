# Alcohol Withdrawal and Delirium Tremens

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and treatment of alcohol withdrawal, withdrawal seizures and delirium tremens, and vitamin replacement, based on the NHS Grampian guideline "Management of acute alcohol withdrawal and medically assisted withdrawal in adults admitted in acute hospitals, with guidance for vitamin replacement" (version 1.1, approved June 2020, amended December 2020). Elective detoxification, children and pregnancy are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Alcohol withdrawal is central nervous system over-excitation after abrupt reduction in alcohol in a dependent drinker. [1]
- Symptoms start 6–12 hours after the last drink, seizures at 12–48 hours, and delirium tremens at 48–96 hours. [1]
- Delirium tremens means severe withdrawal with profound confusion, autonomic overactivity and possible cardiovascular collapse. [1]
- Poor nutrition risks Wernicke encephalopathy from thiamine deficiency. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Tremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea, insomnia, hallucinations, seizures, agitation, confusion. [1]
### Examination signs
- Tremor, tachycardia, hypertension, sweating, fever, agitation; in Wernicke signs: confusion, ataxia, nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia. [1]
### Red flags
- Glasgow Modified Alcohol Withdrawal Scale (GMAWS) score of 9–10. [1]
- Delirium tremens or seizure. [1]
- Features of Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia, reduced Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), hypothermia). [1]
- Significant liver disease. [1]
### Precipitants
- Abrupt cutting down or admission to hospital, trauma, infection, intercurrent illness. [1]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Hepatic encephalopathy | Liver failure, asterixis, raised ammonia |
| Sepsis or meningitis | Fever with focal source or neck stiffness |
| Hypoglycaemia | Low capillary glucose |
| Intoxication with other drugs | Toxidrome and drug screen |
| Wernicke encephalopathy | Ophthalmoplegia and ataxia with poor nutrition |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Alcohol dependence risk | AUDIT score of 20 or above [1] |
| Harmful drinking screen | FAST score of 3 or more [1] |
| Severe withdrawal | GMAWS score 9–10 [1] |
| Significant liver disease (choose lorazepam) | Any of ascites, encephalopathy, albumin below 30 g/L, bilirubin above 50 µmol/L, INR above 1.3 [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Airway, breathing, circulation; capillary glucose; intravenous (IV) access; assess with GMAWS. [1]
- Treat hypoglycaemia first; give IV thiamine (Pabrinex) if Wernicke signs or high risk. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Start chlordiazepoxide by symptom-triggered dosing, or lorazepam in liver disease, age 65 or over or over-sedation risk; delirium tremens: lorazepam. [1]
- Repeat GMAWS at set intervals including overnight. [1]
### Escalation
- Need above maximum dose (chlordiazepoxide 250 mg/day or lorazepam 10 mg/day): senior review, check diagnosis, consider up to 50% more with critical care input. [1]
### Disposition
- Admit patients with delirium tremens, seizures, Wernicke signs, high GMAWS or liver disease. [1]

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | NHS Grampian v1.1 (2020) [1] | Standard UK practice (NICE CG115, not opened) |
|---|---|---|
| First-line benzodiazepine | Chlordiazepoxide, maximum 250 mg/day | Chlordiazepoxide or diazepam |
| Liver disease or age 65 or over | Lorazepam, maximum 10 mg/day | Lorazepam |
| Delirium tremens | Lorazepam | Oral lorazepam, then parenteral if refused |
| Pabrinex | 2 pairs three times daily for 3–5 days if Wernicke signs | Same |
### Discharge criteria
- GMAWS 0 on four consecutive scorings, no seizures, oral thiamine and a withdrawal or addiction plan arranged. [1]
### Follow-up
- Addiction service and general practitioner; liver review if abnormal. [1]
### Patient education
- Do not stop alcohol suddenly without medical help; thiamine for 3 months.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Reduce or stop alcohol, balanced diet, abstinence support.
### Team referrals
- Alcohol liaison; addiction services; hepatology. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Choose the benzodiazepine by liver function and age, and give thiamine correctly.
### Key points
- Give thiamine to anyone at risk of Wernicke encephalopathy. [1]
- Use lorazepam in liver disease or age 65 or over. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Giving glucose before thiamine in suspected Wernicke. [1]
- Missing hypoglycaemia. [1]
- Over-sedation with benzodiazepines. [1]
