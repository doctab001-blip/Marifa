# Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) in Adults

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and first 24 hours of treatment of Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA), based on the Joint British Diabetes Societies (JBDS) 2023 guideline, with the Saudi Ministry of Health (MOH) diabetes emergencies protocol and a review of the 2024 international consensus shown side by side where they differ.

## Pathophysiology
Absolute or relative insulin deficiency with raised counter-regulatory hormones produces hyperglycaemia, ketone production and acidosis.
- Insulin deficiency increases glucose release from the liver and breaks down fat into free fatty acids.
- Free fatty acids are converted to ketone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate), which cause a metabolic acidosis.
- High glucose causes an osmotic diuresis, which produces dehydration and loss of sodium and potassium.
- Acidosis moves potassium out of cells, so the measured potassium can look normal or high while total body potassium is low.
- Sodium-Glucose Co-Transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors can cause DKA with only a mildly raised or normal glucose (euglycaemic DKA). [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Thirst, passing large volumes of urine, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain.
- Weakness, drowsiness or confusion in severe cases.
### Examination signs
- Dehydration with tachycardia and low blood pressure.
- Deep, rapid breathing (Kussmaul breathing) and ketotic breath.
- Reduced conscious level in severe cases.
### Red flags
- Blood ketones above 6.0 mmol/L. [1]
- Venous bicarbonate below 5.0 mmol/L. [1]
- Venous or arterial pH below 7.0. [1]
- Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score below 12 or an abnormal Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive (AVPU) score. [1]
- Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP) below 90 mmHg. [4]
- Serum potassium below 3.5 mmol/L on admission needs senior review and extra potassium. [1]
### Precipitants
- Infection, missed or insufficient insulin, and new-onset type 1 diabetes.
- Acute illness such as myocardial infarction or pancreatitis.
- SGLT2 inhibitor treatment, which can cause euglycaemic DKA. [1]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS) | Glucose very high with osmolality of 320 mOsm/kg or more and little or no ketosis |
| Alcoholic ketoacidosis | Alcohol history with a normal or low glucose |
| Starvation ketosis | Ketones present but pH and bicarbonate are not in the DKA range |
| Lactic acidosis | Raised lactate without significant ketones |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Blood glucose | Above 11.0 mmol/L (about 200 mg/dL); a known history of diabetes also satisfies this criterion in the Saudi MOH protocol and the 2024 consensus, which allows euglycaemic DKA to be recognised [1][2][3] |
| Ketones | Capillary or blood ketones above 3.0 mmol/L, or urine ketones 2+ or more [1][2] |
| Acidosis | Venous bicarbonate below 15.0 mmol/L and/or venous pH below 7.3 [1] |
| Rule | All three criteria must be present [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–60 minutes)
- Confirm all three diagnostic criteria. Start the clock (T=0) when intravenous (IV) fluids begin. [1] [4]
- No IV access: ask for critical care help immediately. [4]
- SBP 90 mmHg or above: give 0.9% sodium chloride 1 L over the first 60 minutes. [1]
- SBP below 90 mmHg: give 0.9% sodium chloride 500 mL over 10–15 minutes and repeat while waiting for senior input; once SBP is above 90 mmHg give 1 L over the next 60 minutes. [1]
- Start Fixed-Rate Intravenous Insulin Infusion (FRIII) at 0.1 units/kg/hour using estimated weight at T=0. [1]
- If a long-acting analogue (glargine, detemir or degludec) is normally taken, continue it at the usual dose and time. [4]
- Give an intramuscular (IM) insulin dose of 0.1 units/kg only if there is a delay in setting up the infusion. [1]
- Check potassium before adding it to fluids (see Medicines). [1]
- Measure capillary glucose and ketones hourly; vital signs and National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2) hourly after the first hour; record fluid balance with a minimum urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/hour. [4]
- Stop any SGLT2 inhibitor, complete a Yellow Card report and treat euglycaemic DKA in exactly the same way. [1]
### Ongoing (1–24 hours)
- Aim for a ketone fall of 0.5 mmol/L per hour, a bicarbonate rise of 3.0 mmol/L per hour and a glucose fall of 3.0 mmol/L per hour. [1]
- When glucose falls below 14 mmol/L, add 10% glucose at 125 mL/hour and consider reducing insulin to 0.05 units/kg/hour. [1]
- Continue 0.9% sodium chloride 1 L with potassium over 2 hours, 2 hours, 4 hours, 4 hours, then 6 hours. Reassess cardiovascular status at 12 hours; more fluid may be needed. [1]
- Use more cautious fluid replacement in people aged 18–25 years, older adults, pregnancy and heart or renal failure. [1]
- Do not give bicarbonate routinely. [1]
- Give prophylactic Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH) as per National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance. [1]
- At 6 hours check venous pH, bicarbonate, potassium, ketones and glucose. If DKA has not resolved, check the pump is working and connected and that the correct insulin volume is loaded, then review the infusion. [4]
- DKA is resolved when ketones are below 0.6 mmol/L and venous pH is above 7.3. Do not use bicarbonate to judge resolution. [1]
- Keep the insulin infusion running until the person is eating and drinking normally. If ketones have cleared but the person is not eating, change to a Variable-Rate Intravenous Insulin Infusion (VRIII) per local guidance. [1] [4]
- Change to subcutaneous insulin only when ketones are below 0.6 mmol/L, pH is above 7.3 and the person is eating and drinking. Do not stop the infusion until 30 minutes after subcutaneous short-acting insulin is given. [1] [4]
### Escalation
- Severe DKA markers: ketones above 6.0 mmol/L, bicarbonate below 5.0 mmol/L, pH below 7.0, or GCS below 12 or abnormal AVPU. Involve senior and critical care teams. [1]
- SBP below 90 mmHg: consider critical care and senior input; consider other causes such as heart failure or sepsis. [4]
- Not improving against the ketone, bicarbonate and glucose targets: seek senior advice. [1] [4]
- Watch for fluid overload and cerebral oedema; consider High Dependency Unit (HDU) care and a central line in high-risk groups. [4]
- Refer to the Diabetes Specialist Team (DST) within one working day of admission. [1]

### Discharge criteria
- Ketones below 0.6 mmol/L, venous pH above 7.3, eating and drinking normally and converted to subcutaneous insulin. [1]
- Newly diagnosed diabetes: seen by the specialist team before discharge. [1]
### Follow-up
- Refer to the DST within one working day of admission and review within 24 hours. [1]
- Discharge with a written care plan copied to the General Practitioner (GP) and an out-of-hours contact number. [1]
### Patient education
- Written sick-day rules and when to seek help. [1]
- Home ketone meters should be the default. [1]
### Lifestyle and diet
- Regular glucose and ketone monitoring and carbohydrate-aware eating, guided by the diabetes team. [1]
### Team referrals
- Diabetes Specialist Team within one working day; critical care for severe DKA or no IV access. [1] [4]
### Learning objectives
- Recognise DKA using all three criteria and grade severity.
- Start fluids and a fixed-rate insulin infusion promptly and manage potassium safely.
- Know when to escalate and when DKA has resolved.
### Key points
- All three criteria (glucose, ketones, acidosis) must be present; euglycaemic DKA exists on SGLT2 inhibitors. [1]
- Fluids and fixed-rate insulin start together at T=0. [1]
- Keep insulin running with glucose until the person is eating and drinking. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Stopping insulin when glucose normalises while ketones are still raised. [1]
- Using bicarbonate to judge resolution instead of ketones and pH. [1]
- Missing hypokalaemia (below 3.5 mmol/L) before insulin has taken effect. [1]
- Giving fast fluids to young adults aged 18–25 years, older adults, pregnant patients or those with heart or renal failure without senior review. [1]
