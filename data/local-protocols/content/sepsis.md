# Sepsis in Adults

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and first-hour management of sepsis and septic shock of unknown source, based on the NHS "Management of sepsis of unknown source in adult patients" (issued 1 October 2018, United Lincolnshire Hospitals NHS Trust) with fluid and vasopressor values from standard UK practice (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) NG51 and Surviving Sepsis Campaign, not opened) and the UK summary of product characteristics for noradrenaline. Neutropenic sepsis, meningitis, pregnancy and children are outside this protocol (see the separate protocols). No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. [1]
- Vasodilatation, capillary leak and myocardial depression lower tissue perfusion and raise lactate. [1]
- Septic shock is sepsis with persistent hypotension needing vasopressors and lactate above 2 mmol/L despite fluid. [1]
- Early antibiotics and source control improve survival; each hour of delay raises mortality. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Fever or rigors, malaise, confusion, breathlessness, reduced urine, symptoms of a source (cough, dysuria, abdominal pain). [1]
### Examination signs
- Tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypotension, altered mental state, warm or cold peripheries, mottled skin, reduced urine output. [1]
### Red flags
- Systolic blood pressure (SBP) 90 mmHg or below, or mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg. [1]
- Lactate above 2 mmol/L (4 mmol/L or above is high risk). [1]
- Altered consciousness, oxygen need, urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/hour. [1]
- Non-blanching rash. [1]
### Precipitants
- Pneumonia, urinary tract infection, abdominal infection, skin and soft tissue infection, line infection; immunosuppression, diabetes, extremes of age. [1]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Hypovolaemic shock | Haemorrhage or fluid loss without fever or infection source |
| Cardiogenic shock | Raised jugular venous pressure, low output on echocardiography |
| Anaphylaxis | Rapid onset after trigger with skin or airway swelling |
| Adrenal crisis | Known steroid use or adrenal disease, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia |
| Diabetic ketoacidosis | Ketones and glucose above 11 mmol/L |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Sepsis | Suspected infection with organ dysfunction (high National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2), lactate above 2 mmol/L, altered consciousness) [1] |
| Septic shock | Sepsis needing vasopressor to keep mean arterial pressure at or above 65 mmHg and lactate above 2 mmol/L despite fluid [1] |

| Test | Normal (adult) | In this condition | What it means / next step | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Computed tomography or ultrasound for source | No collection | Abscess, obstructed infected kidney, perforation | Urgent source control | [1] |
| Critical care and microbiology | Not needed | Septic shock or no response by 48 hours | Escalate; contact the consultant microbiologist | [1] section Review |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Recognise; give oxygen to keep saturation 94–98%; intravenous (IV) access; take blood cultures and lactate. [1]
- Start broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour of presentation (stat meropenem in septic shock). [1]
- Fluid bolus for hypotension or lactate above 2 mmol/L. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Reassess after each bolus; vasopressor if mean arterial pressure stays below 65 mmHg. [1]
- Urinary catheter and hourly urine output; source control. [1]
### Escalation
- Septic shock or persistent lactate above 4 mmol/L: critical care. [1]
### Disposition
- Admit all sepsis; critical care for shock. [1]

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | Lincolnshire sepsis guideline (2018) [1] | UK standard practice (NICE NG51 / Surviving Sepsis, not opened) [3] |
|---|---|---|
| Antibiotic timing | Within 1 hour; stat meropenem in shock | Within 1 hour for high risk |
| Fluid | Not given in the extract | 500 mL bolus; up to 30 mL/kg |
| First-line antibiotic | Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g every 8 hours | Per source |
| Review | 24–48 hours; at least 2 days IV | 24–48 hours |
### Discharge criteria
- Not for ED discharge when sepsis criteria are met; step down after clinical response. [1]
### Follow-up
- Microbiology review of cultures; follow-up for source treatment. [1]
### Patient education
- Understand the infection and the signs of recurrence.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Vaccinations; manage diabetes; good wound and catheter care.
### Team referrals
- Critical care; microbiology; source-control surgeon or radiologist. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Recognise sepsis, give the one-hour bundle and recognise septic shock.
### Key points
- Blood cultures, lactate, antibiotics and fluid within 1 hour. [1]
- Review antibiotics at 24–48 hours. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Delaying antibiotics for cultures. [1]
- Aminoglycoside in acute kidney injury. [1]
- Missing a source needing drainage. [1]
