# Hyponatraemia

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) emergency treatment of severe or moderately severe symptomatic hyponatraemia, based on the Society for Endocrinology 2022 emergency guidance and a Royal Devon acute medicine pathway. Chronic asymptomatic hyponatraemia and the full cause work-up are outside this protocol. No Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Hyponatraemia is a low serum sodium that reflects an excess of water relative to sodium. [1]
- Causes include diuretics, the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, heart failure, cirrhosis, vomiting or diarrhoea, and excess water intake.
- A fall in sodium moves water into brain cells; brain swelling causes headache, vomiting, seizures and coma. [1]
- Over-correction can cause osmotic demyelination, so the rate of correction is limited. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Nausea without vomiting, headache, confusion (moderately severe). [1]
- Persistent vomiting, seizures, reduced consciousness (severe). [1]
### Examination signs
- Volume status: dry, normal or fluid overloaded; reduced Glasgow Coma Scale; seizure.
### Red flags
- Cardiorespiratory arrest, seizures or Glasgow Coma Scale of 8 or less. [1]
- Persistent vomiting. [1]
- Severe symptoms with sodium above 130 mmol/L suggest another neurological cause. [1]
### Precipitants
- Thiazide diuretics, antidepressants, carbamazepine, adrenal crisis, water loading, marathon running, ecstasy.
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Pseudohyponatraemia | Normal measured osmolality (very high lipids or protein) |
| Hyperglycaemic hyponatraemia | Very high glucose; sodium rises as glucose falls |
| Adrenal insufficiency | Hypotension, hyperkalaemia, low cortisol |
| Stroke or intracranial cause | Focal signs with sodium above 130 mmol/L |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Mild | Sodium 130–135 mmol/L [1] |
| Moderate | Sodium 125–129 mmol/L [1] |
| Profound | Sodium below 125 mmol/L [1] |
| Severe symptoms | Vomiting, seizures, coma, arrest [1] |
| Moderately severe symptoms | Nausea, confusion, headache [1] |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Assess symptoms and airway, breathing, circulation and consciousness. [1]
- Severe symptoms: give an intravenous bolus of hypertonic saline without waiting for the cause. [1]
- Moderately severe symptoms: senior review; hypertonic saline is for severe symptoms and moderately severe cases per the 2022 guidance. [1]
- Aim for an initial rise of 5 mmol/L in the first hour. [1]
- Do not use vaptans or the Adrogue–Madias formula. [1]
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- If no improvement after the first rise, give one more bolus aiming for a further 1 mmol/L rise. [1]
- Check sodium at 6 hours, 12 hours and then daily until stable. [1]
- Monitor urine output; a rise can signal rapid correction. [1]
### Escalation
- Over-correction (more than 10 mmol/L in 24 hours, or 8 mmol/L per 24 hours after that): stop hypertonic fluid and consult an experienced clinician. [1]
- Options for over-correction include hypotonic fluid to limit the rise or re-lower sodium, with or without parenteral desmopressin; the 2022 guidance gives no dose. [1]
- Royal Devon uses 5% dextrose at 125 mL/hour if the rise exceeds 10 mmol/24 hours. [2]
### Disposition
- Admit to a monitored setting with senior supervision. [1]

### Discharge criteria
- Sodium at or above 130 mmol/L and rising safely, symptoms resolved and cause treated. [1]
### Follow-up
- Repeat sodium within days; cause-specific review (medicines, endocrine, renal). [1]
### Patient education
- Avoid excess water intake; report new confusion, vomiting or seizure.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Fluid and diet advice according to the cause.
### Team referrals
- Endocrinology or nephrology for persistent or unexplained cases; critical care for coma or arrest. [1]
### Learning objectives
- Grade hyponatraemia by symptoms and biochemistry.
- Give a hypertonic bolus safely and recognise over-correction.
### Key points
- Treat severe symptoms with a 150 mL bolus of 3% saline over 20 minutes. [1]
- Never exceed 10 mmol/L rise in 24 hours. [1]
### Pitfalls
- Using continuous hypertonic infusion instead of boluses. [1]
- Using vaptans or the Adrogue–Madias formula. [1]
- Missing over-correction after recovery from adrenal crisis. [1]
