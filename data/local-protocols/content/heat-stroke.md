# Heat Stroke

SCOPE: Adult (over 18 years) Emergency Department (ED) recognition and emergency cooling of classic and exertional heat stroke, with supportive medicine treatment, based on the Society of Critical Care Medicine Guideline for the Treatment of Heat Stroke (Critical Care Medicine 2025) and the Emergency Care BC heat-related illness summary (updated 9 July 2024). Heat exhaustion, malignant hyperthermia and drug-induced hyperthermia management are outside this protocol. This is a hot-climate priority for Saudi Arabia (Hajj, outdoor work, summer); no Saudi national guideline was found.

## Pathophysiology
Heat stroke is failure of thermoregulation, with core temperature above 40 °C and central nervous system dysfunction, causing multi-organ injury. [1] [2]
- Classic heat stroke follows environmental heat exposure in older, chronically ill or dehydrated patients; exertional heat stroke follows strenuous exercise or work in the heat in young fit patients. [2]
- Injury to the brain, liver, kidney, muscle and coagulation system worsens with the time spent above the target temperature, so cooling speed determines outcome. [1]
- Antipyretics do not lower temperature in heat illness because the set point is normal, and may add to organ injury. [2]
- Cooling rate matters more than the method chosen: immersion in cold water is fastest. [1]

## Signs & Symptoms
### Symptoms
- Collapse, confusion, headache, nausea, vomiting, muscle cramps, seizure, loss of consciousness after heat exposure or exertion. [2]
### Examination signs
- Core (rectal) temperature above 40 °C, altered mental state, hot skin (sweating may persist in exertional cases), tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnoea. [2]
### Red flags
- Core temperature above 40 °C with any central nervous system abnormality (confusion, ataxia, seizure, coma). [2]
- Seizure or Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) below 13. [2]
- Hypotension or signs of multi-organ failure. [2]
- Dark urine or muscle pain suggesting rhabdomyolysis. [2]
### Precipitants
- Heat waves, outdoor work, pilgrimage, sport, dehydration, older age, diuretics, anticholinergic drugs, stimulants. [2]
### Differentials
| Condition | The one feature that separates it |
|---|---|
| Sepsis | Source of infection, raised inflammatory markers, no heat exposure |
| Thyroid storm | Known thyroid disease, goitre, very high thyroid hormones |
| Serotonin syndrome | Serotonergic drug, clonus and hyperreflexia |
| Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | Antipsychotic drug, rigidity, slow onset over days |
| Aspirin overdose | Raised salicylate level, tinnitus, metabolic acidosis |
| Sympathomimetic toxicity | Stimulant use, dilated pupils |

## Investigations
### Diagnostic criteria
| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Heat stroke | Core (rectal) temperature above 40 °C with central nervous system abnormality [2] section Diagnosis |
| Cooling target | Core temperature below 39 °C (range 38–39 °C) [1] section Cooling; [2] section Treatment |
| Time to target | Within 30 minutes of recognition [1] section Cooling |
| Preferred cooling rate | At least 0.155 °C per minute [1] section Cooling |

## Management
### Immediate (0–15 minutes)
- Move out of the heat, remove clothing, secure airway, breathing and circulation, intravenous (IV) access, and insert a rectal temperature probe. [2]
- Start active cooling at once: cold-water immersion (9–12 °C) or ice-water immersion (1–5 °C) is the fastest method. [1] section Cooling
- If immersion is not possible: ice packs over the whole body, evaporative cooling (tepid spray with fans) and cold IV fluids (4 °C). [2] section Treatment
### Ongoing (15–60 minutes)
- Aim to reach below 39 °C within 30 minutes, then stop active cooling to avoid overshoot to hypothermia and keep monitoring rectal temperature. [1] section Cooling
- Treat agitation, shivering and seizures; correct glucose and electrolytes; check for rhabdomyolysis and kidney injury. [2]
- Do not give antipyretics (paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, aspirin) or dantrolene. [1] section Drugs to avoid
### Escalation
- Temperature not controlled, seizures, shock or organ failure: critical care; consider inotropes and extracorporeal support where available. [2]
### Disposition
- Admit all patients with heat stroke. Exertional heat stroke that resolves fully after cooling may be discharged after up to 6 hours of observation on senior decision only. [2] section Disposition

### Guideline comparison
| Parameter | SCCM guideline (2025) [1] | Emergency Care BC summary (2024) [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Cooling method | Active over passive; ice-water (1–5 °C) or cold-water (9–12 °C) immersion fastest | Cold-water immersion preferred; evaporative or convective if not available |
| Target | Below 39 °C within 30 minutes of recognition | 38–39 °C ideally within 30 minutes |
| Dantrolene | Recommended against | Not recommended routinely |
| Antipyretics | Avoid | Avoid |
| Fluids | Not covered | Cold IV fluids (4 °C) as adjunct; crystalloids for rhabdomyolysis |
### Discharge criteria
- Fully recovered neurological state, normal temperature and normal creatine kinase, kidney function and liver function; senior sign-off after at least 6 hours of observation (exertional only). [2]
### Follow-up
- General practitioner review in 1 week; sports or occupational health advice on return to exertion; repeat creatinine and liver function. [2]
### Patient education
- Heat stroke can recur; avoid heat exposure and strenuous exercise until cleared; drink fluids regularly in hot weather.
### Lifestyle and diet
- Gradual acclimatisation over 1–2 weeks, adequate hydration, light clothing and shade breaks during work or pilgrimage.
### Team referrals
- Critical care; nephrology if kidney injury; sports medicine or occupational health. [2]
### Learning objectives
- Recognise heat stroke by temperature and mental state, start immersion cooling immediately and avoid antipyretics and dantrolene.
### Key points
- Cool first, below 39 °C within 30 minutes. [1]
- Use a rectal temperature probe. [2]
### Pitfalls
- Waiting for blood tests before cooling. [1]
- Giving paracetamol or dantrolene. [1]
- Missing rhabdomyolysis and kidney injury. [2]
